# Portfolio site research: AI, data, product, and software engineering

**Research checked:** 25 October 2026  
**Purpose:** A curated set of real personal sites and portfolio platforms to study when building a portfolio for data science, AI engineering, forward-deployed engineering, full-stack development, AI architecture, or product architecture/engineering.

“Best” is subjective. This is a practical shortlist, not a universal ranking: examples were selected for clear positioning, useful proof of work, or strong product/technical storytelling. Individual sites can change over time; treat them as inspiration, not templates to copy.

## 1. Data scientists

- **[Julia Silge](https://juliasilge.com/)** — A focused practitioner profile combining data science and software engineering, technical writing, visualization, speaking, and real-world machine learning. Useful model for communicating technical work to more than one audience.
- **[Rob J. Hyndman](https://robjhyndman.com/)** — An academic/statistics profile with research, teaching, and resources. Study how a specialist can make expertise and substantial written work easy to navigate.
- **[Eugene Yan](https://eugeneyan.com/)** — Especially useful for applied data science: connects recommender systems and machine learning to prototypes, product questions, and implementation details.
- **[Ryan Liwag — Visual Datos](https://ryanliwag.com/)** — A data scientist and data designer whose portfolio includes an animated, scroll-driven earthquake story; one of the closest matches here for combining real data work with a distinctive visual experience.
- **[Nadieh Bremer — Visual Cinnamon](https://www.visualcinnamon.com/)** — A data visualization designer with a data-science background. Study her bespoke interactive and animated visualizations, and how she explains the process behind them.
- **[fast.ai](https://www.fast.ai/)** — An education and research organization rather than an individual portfolio. Its [team page](https://www.fast.ai/about.html) and courses are useful examples of making applied deep learning accessible through teaching, software, research, and community.
- **[Kaggle](https://www.kaggle.com/)** — A portfolio platform rather than an individual site. Use it to publish notebooks, competition work, datasets, and reproducible analyses; link the strongest projects from a personal homepage.

**What to show:** a small number of complete analyses; the question and intended decision; data provenance and limitations; reproducible notebooks or code; a clear chart or demo; and measurable findings where appropriate. Explain what you would and would not conclude from the data.

## 3. Forward-deployed engineers (FDEs)

Dedicated FDE portfolios are still a smaller set, and customer work may be confidential. The following personal sites make the customer-facing engineering angle visible in different ways. Their experience and project claims are self-reported; evaluate the presentation patterns with that context. **Do not disclose customer data, internal architecture, credentials, or other confidential details.**

- **[Othmane Zizi](https://othmanezizi.com/)** — Explicitly positions around full-stack AI and forward-deployed engineering. The deployment log, stack layers, and field context make the path from customer problem to shipped system easy to scan; useful for an interactive FDE portfolio.
- **[Jean Khawand](https://www.jeankhawand.com/)** — A forward-deployed engineer whose site connects reliability work, integrations, technical writing, and an interactive portfolio. Useful for showing investigation and operational problem-solving, not only project launches.
- **[Kris Diallo](https://krisdiallo.com/)** — A forward-deployed engineer’s profile and writing hub. Study the clear role statement and career narrative, then support that framing with detailed, sanitized project evidence.

### Role context and related engineering examples

- **[Palantir Careers](https://www.palantir.com/careers/)** — Employer role context. Check the current role description instead of relying on old job-post URLs or third-party summaries.
- **[Palantir Foundry documentation](https://www.palantir.com/docs/foundry/)** — Product documentation that gives context on enterprise data integration, model operationalization, and workflow surfaces.
- **[Eugene Yan](https://eugeneyan.com/)** — A useful public example of explaining applied work end to end with prototypes, writing, and implementation details.
- **[Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/)** — Useful model for documenting technical investigations and outcomes in a public, reproducible way.

For an FDE portfolio, use **sanitized case studies** instead of customer name-dropping. Each case study should cover:

1. The user or operational problem (generalized if necessary).
2. Constraints: existing systems, permissions, data quality, latency, deployment environment, or change-management needs.
3. Your role and the decisions you made, distinct from the wider team’s work.
4. The integration and delivery approach, with a redacted or synthetic diagram if needed.
5. Adoption, reliability, time saved, or another defensible outcome; explain how it was measured.
6. What changed after customer feedback and what you learned.

When a detail cannot be shared, say so plainly and show a synthetic example that demonstrates the same engineering skill without implying it is the customer's production system.

## 6. Product architects and product engineers — highest priority

“Product architect” is not a consistently defined job title. A strong portfolio should make the intended meaning obvious: **product judgment plus technical architecture plus evidence of delivery**. For product engineering roles, show how you work backward from user needs to shipped software.

- **[Lee Robinson — Product Engineers](https://leerob.com/product-engineers)** — Directly relevant guide to product engineering: user experience, broad technical range, iteration, customer focus, and pragmatic choices. Pair the article with [Lee’s personal site](https://leerob.com/) as an example of a portfolio around an engineering identity.
- **[Eugene Yan](https://eugeneyan.com/)** — Strong example of connecting AI/ML product ideas to prototypes, implementation notes, and technical writing.
- **[Hamel Husain](https://hamel.dev/)** — Particularly relevant to AI product engineering: his writing connects evals, engineering, and product decisions, illustrating how to make quality measurement part of the product story.
- **[Emil Kowalski](https://emilkowal.ski/)** — Particularly strong for the craft and execution side: presents products/components alongside the reasoning and implementation details behind them.
- **[Rauno Freiberg](https://rauno.me/)** — Useful inspiration for a distinctive design-engineering identity and visual polish; supplement this style with clear outcome-focused product case studies.
- **[swyx](https://swyx.io/)** — Useful for positioning a technical career around emerging fields, public learning, writing, and talks. His AI-engineering writing also connects product-building practice with technical trends.
- **[Pieter Levels](https://levels.io/)** — Founder-builder example: a personal site centered on products built and the practice of building internet businesses. Study the product-first framing and concise maker identity; do not treat it as a corporate case-study template.
- **[Brittany Chiang](https://brittanychiang.com/)** — Useful for showing product quality through accessible interfaces, design-system work, and cross-functional engineering.
- **[Product Talk](https://www.producttalk.org/)** — A product-discovery resource rather than an individual engineering portfolio. Its material on outcomes, customer interviews, and opportunity-solution trees can inform how to explain product decisions.
- **[Product Hunt](https://www.producthunt.com/)** — A launch/discovery platform, not a personal portfolio. Useful as supplementary evidence of a launch or maker profile, but it does not replace a case study showing your contribution and what happened after launch.

### What the product case study should make easy to judge

1. **Problem and user:** who had the problem, what evidence showed it mattered, and what outcome you targeted.
2. **Product judgment:** what you chose to build first, what you deliberately left out, and why.
3. **Architecture:** the system boundaries, important data flows, integrations, and trade-offs behind the experience.
4. **Execution:** your specific contribution, collaborators, iterations, and how you handled constraints.
5. **Result:** adoption, task success, retention, revenue, quality, or another relevant measure, with timeframe and context.
6. **Learning:** what feedback or evidence changed your original view and what you would improve next.

For a combined product-architect/product-engineer profile, lead with two or three end-to-end case studies. Each should connect a real user need to a product decision, system design, shipped experience, and outcome. Use architecture diagrams and product documents as supporting evidence, not as a substitute for the story.

### Direction for a product architect / engineer portfolio

Make the project story the experience: **user need → product decision → system design → shipped interface → measured result**. A pinned or scroll-linked diagram can reveal the data flow and trade-offs as the reader moves through the case study. Keep the page usable without animation, retain ordinary navigation, and provide a reduced-motion path. For AI and data work, label the dataset, evaluation, and limitations beside the animated result. For FDE work, use a clearly labeled synthetic or approved diagram when the real customer system is confidential.

For a useful benchmark set, study **Lee Robinson** for product-engineering positioning, **Eugene Yan** and **Hamel Husain** for AI product evidence, **Emil Kowalski** for interface motion, and **Ryan Liwag** for scroll-led data storytelling.

### What these references informed in the portfolio

- The homepage now opens with a full-bleed charcoal hero, larger product-engineering positioning, and a contrasting featured case-study panel. This gives the work a deliberate launch-story rhythm while keeping the palette and diagrams original.
- All four case studies now open with a full-bleed, color-tinted system-story header. The numbered flow and role/status details sit together before the chapter rail, so the reader gets product context before the deeper narrative.
- The home page keeps the product-engineering story and project routes direct while using the existing work explorer and custom system diagram to connect capabilities to relevant case studies.
- The hero work explorer now responds to a fine pointer with a restrained perspective tilt, moving scan traces, and a light field that follows the pointer. This takes a cue from Aevion's immersive technical presentation while keeping the map light, keyboard reachable, and free of fabricated project metrics.
- Scroll-linked visual sequences make selected project artifacts and the product-delivery steps unfold as readers move down the page. The effects remain secondary to the content and honor reduced-motion preferences.
- Case-study pages pair direct section links with a sticky chapter count and progress line that follows the active section. This brings the references' strong wayfinding into the technical stories without copying their art direction.
- The home-page work list now has its own numbered four-project chapter rail, and the hero explorer has an animated scan treatment. This is the navigation layer for a larger “Product Systems Atlas” redesign, not the final 3D scene.
- Hover and keyboard focus reveal the same project summaries; ordinary links remain available, and the motion opt-in supports readers whose system setting requests reduced motion.
- The next design increment is one carefully scoped 3D system model, paired with a role-lens profile animation. Project evidence, ownership, and publishable artifacts remain the content dependency; no 3D scene should imply confidential client architecture or unsupported impact.

## Cross-role portfolio checklist

- State the role you are targeting and what kind of problems you solve.
- Put the strongest two or three projects near the top; prefer depth over a long, undifferentiated project list.
- Explain each project in plain language before introducing tools or acronyms.
- Separate your individual contribution from team outcomes.
- Link to a working demo, code, writing, or design artifact where sharing is appropriate.
- Include meaningful evidence: users, measurements, benchmarks, reliability, or adoption—with context.
- Show trade-offs, limitations, and lessons, not just a polished success narrative.
- Make contact details and a short resume/CV easy to find; make the site fast, accessible, and usable on mobile.
- Remove secrets and confidential employer/customer information; use redacted or synthetic examples when needed.
